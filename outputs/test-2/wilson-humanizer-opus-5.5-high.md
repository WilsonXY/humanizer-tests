# AI-Assisted Writing: Essential Guidelines

## How AI detectors work

AI detectors try to estimate whether a piece of writing looks like something a language model produced. A big part of that is predictability, meaning how likely each word choice is given the words around it. They also look at the shape of the writing, such as how long the sentences are, how they're built, what vocabulary shows up and how often things repeat. Grammar, phrasing and overall consistency feed into the result as well. On top of that, detectors use classifier scores, which come from patterns a model has learned by comparing examples of human and AI writing.

The catch is that no detector is perfectly reliable. Human writing can get flagged as AI, and AI writing can go through without being noticed. A detector score is an estimate, so it shouldn't be read as proof of who wrote something.

## Principles for AI-assisted writing

Good AI-assisted writing starts with your own ideas. Before you open any tool, you should already have the arguments, opinions, notes and examples you want to work with. The AI can help you shape that material, but the material itself should come from you.

Your voice matters just as much. Keep the wording and tone close to how you normally communicate, so the finished piece still sounds like you. If the AI suggests a sentence you would never actually say, it's worth rewriting it in your own words.

Substance should come before style. An argument needs to be specific, relevant to the question and logical, and it should be supported by evidence. Generic writing works against all of this, so cut the repetitive explanations, the transitions that don't connect anything, and the claims that are too vague to check.

After that, verify everything. Check the facts and citations, along with any assumptions the AI added on its own, before they end up in your final version. It also helps to keep your drafts. Notes and revision history show how the piece developed, and they act as evidence of your writing process if anyone asks about it. Finally, follow the rules that apply to you. If your university or workplace has a policy on AI assistance, that policy decides what is allowed.

## Recommended workflow

The workflow itself is fairly simple. Write down your rough ideas first, even if they're messy. Then ask the AI to organize them and make them clearer. Go through each change it makes and correct anything that misrepresents what you actually think. Once that's done, edit the text yourself for clarity and accuracy, and make sure it sounds the way you intended. The last step is to check the final version one more time and hold on to your drafts.

## A reusable prompt

The prompt below can be pasted into an AI tool at the start of an editing session. It asks the tool to work on how the writing reads while leaving your ideas and reasoning alone.

> Help me improve my writing while preserving my original ideas, reasoning, and intended voice. Improve clarity, structure, grammar, and readability. Do not invent facts, examples, experiences, or references. Avoid generic wording, unnecessary repetition, and excessive formality. Preserve nuance and do not change my intended meaning. Flag unsupported claims and ask questions when information is missing. Explain significant changes when useful. Prioritize accurate, natural, substantive writing over stylistic polish. Do not guarantee or optimize for any AI detector score.

The last line of the prompt is there on purpose. The aim is writing that is accurate and reads naturally, and a detector score doesn't measure that reliably.

## Bottom line

Use AI to help you put your thinking into words, while the thinking itself stays yours. Deliberately adding mistakes or swapping words at random to change a detection score doesn't work reliably, and it can make the writing worse.
